AI TEACH ENGLISH — 12 TENSES MASTER KNOWLEDGE BASE Version: 1.0 Purpose:
Grounding knowledge for the AI English Teacher Scope: The traditional
12-form teaching framework used in English-learning courses

======================================================================
0. AI TEACHER OPERATING INSTRUCTIONS
======================================================================

ROLE You are a patient, accurate, practical English grammar teacher.
Your job is to help learners understand and use English tense forms
correctly, not merely memorize formulas.

TEACHING RULES 1. Answer the learner’s actual question first. 2. Use
this knowledge base as the primary grammar reference. 3. Explain in
simple English by default. If the learner asks for another language or a
simpler explanation, adapt accordingly. 4. Always distinguish FORM from
USE: - FORM = how the verb phrase is constructed. - USE = why a speaker
chooses that form in context. 5. Give natural, modern examples. 6. When
correcting a sentence: a) show the learner’s sentence, b) give the
corrected sentence, c) explain the reason briefly, d) give one or two
comparable examples. 7. If more than one tense is grammatically
possible, do not pretend there is only one answer. Explain how the
meaning changes. 8. Do not invent a grammar rule merely to justify an
answer. 9. Do not treat signal words as automatic tense selectors. Words
such as “since”, “already”, “while”, “by”, and “tomorrow” are clues;
context determines the form. 10. Remember that stative verbs are not
normally used in continuous forms when they describe states (e.g. know,
believe, own, need, understand), though some verbs can be dynamic with a
different meaning. 11. When the learner asks for practice, generate
fresh examples rather than repeating the examples in this file. 12. When
generating a quiz, keep each question unambiguous and check the answer
before presenting it. 13. Never shame a learner for mistakes. 14. Prefer
useful explanation over terminology. Introduce technical terms only when
they help. 15. If the learner’s intended meaning is unclear, explain the
likely interpretations.

IMPORTANT TERMINOLOGY NOTE Many teaching courses organize English into
“12 tenses”: four present forms, four past forms, and four future forms.
This is a useful learning framework and is used throughout this
knowledge base. In stricter linguistic descriptions, English is often
analysed as having present and past tense forms, while future time is
expressed through constructions such as “will” plus aspect. If a learner
asks about this distinction, explain it calmly; do not tell them that
the 12-form learning framework is useless or “wrong”.

VERB FORM KEY V1 = base form: work, go, write V2 = past form: worked,
went, wrote V3 = past participle: worked, gone, written V-ing = -ing
form/present participle: working, going, writing

CORE ASPECT IDEA SIMPLE: presents a fact, state, habit, event, or
complete situation without highlighting its internal duration.
CONTINUOUS: views an activity as in progress, temporary, developing, or
unfolding. PERFECT: connects one time to an earlier event/state; often
gives a “looking back” view. PERFECT CONTINUOUS: combines an earlier
starting point with emphasis on duration/activity.

====================================================================== 1.
PRESENT SIMPLE
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Present Simple mainly for facts, states, habits,
routines, repeated events, general truths, and certain scheduled future
events.

FORM Affirmative: I/You/We/They + V1 He/She/It + V1-s/es

Negative: I/You/We/They + do not (don’t) + V1 He/She/It + does not
(doesn’t) + V1

Question: Do + I/you/we/they + V1 …? Does + he/she/it + V1 …?

BE: I am; You/We/They are; He/She/It is Negative: am not / are not / is
not Question: Am/Are/Is + subject …?

MAIN USES 1. Habits and routines: I walk to work every morning. She
studies after dinner. 2. General truths and facts: Water boils at 100°C
at standard atmospheric pressure. The Earth moves around the Sun. 3.
States: I know the answer. He owns a small apartment. 4. Repeated
events: The shop closes at six every day. 5. Instructions,
demonstrations, commentary and summaries: First, you open the file and
select the image. 6. Timetabled/scheduled future events: The train
leaves at 7:30 tomorrow. The course starts next Monday. 7. Future time
clauses after words such as when, after, before, until, as soon as: I’ll
call you when I arrive. We won’t leave until the rain stops.

COMMON TIME EXPRESSIONS always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely,
never, every day, on Mondays, once a week, generally, normally.

THIRD-PERSON SPELLING work -> works watch -> watches go -> goes study ->
studies have -> has

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: She work every day. Correct: She works every day.

Wrong: He doesn’t works here. Correct: He doesn’t work here. Reason:
“doesn’t” already carries the third-person marking; use V1 after it.

Wrong: Does she likes coffee? Correct: Does she like coffee?

Wrong: I am knowing him. Correct: I know him. Reason: “know” is normally
stative in this meaning.

CONTRAST Present Simple: I work from home. = normal/routine situation.
Present Continuous: I am working from home this week. =
temporary/current situation.

TEACHER CHECK Ask: Is this a habit/fact/state/repeated event, or is the
speaker emphasizing an activity in progress? This often separates
Present Simple from Present Continuous.

======================================================================
2. PRESENT CONTINUOUS
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Present Continuous to view an activity or situation
as happening around now, temporary, developing, changing, or arranged
for the future.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + am/is/are + V-ing I am working. She is
working. They are working.

Negative: Subject + am/is/are + not + V-ing

Question: Am/Is/Are + subject + V-ing …?

MAIN USES 1. Happening at the moment of speaking: Please be quiet. I’m
studying. 2. Happening around the present period: I’m reading a very
interesting book this week. 3. Temporary situations: She is staying with
her sister for a month. 4. Changing/developing situations: Your English
is improving. Prices are rising. 5. Repeated behaviour with words such
as “always”, often showing emotion: He’s always losing his keys. 6.
Personal future arrangements: I’m meeting Sara tomorrow at ten. We’re
flying to Rome on Friday.

SPELLING NOTES make -> making write -> writing run -> running sit ->
sitting lie -> lying

STATIVE VERBS Continuous forms are normally avoided when a verb
describes a state: know, understand, believe, want, need, own, belong,
remember, prefer, love, hate, seem.

Correct: I understand the problem. Usually not: I am understanding the
problem.

MEANING CAN CHANGE I think you’re right. = believe/opinion; state I’m
thinking about the problem. = mental activity/process

I have a car. = possession I’m having lunch. = activity

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: She working now. Correct: She is working now.

Wrong: They are play football. Correct: They are playing football.

Wrong: I am wanting a new phone. Correct: I want a new phone.

CONTRAST Present Simple: She teaches English. = regular
occupation/activity. Present Continuous: She is teaching English this
semester. = current/temporary situation.

======================================================================
3. PRESENT PERFECT SIMPLE
======================================================================

CORE MEANING The Present Perfect connects an earlier event or state with
the present. The exact past time is often unspecified or the time period
is still open.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + have/has + V3 I have finished. She has
finished.

Negative: Subject + have/has + not + V3

Question: Have/Has + subject + V3 …?

MAIN USES 1. Past action with a present result: I’ve lost my keys. = I
do not have them now. 2. Life experience up to now: Have you ever
visited Canada? I’ve never tried skiing. 3. Events in an unfinished time
period: I’ve had two meetings today. = “today” is viewed as unfinished.
4. States that began in the past and continue now: I’ve known her for
ten years. He has lived here since 2020. 5. Recent events: She’s just
arrived. 6. Achievements/changes up to the present: Scientists have
discovered many new species.

COMMON WORDS ever, never, already, yet, just, recently, lately, so far,
until now, since, for, this week, today.

SINCE AND FOR since + starting point: since Monday, since 2020, since I
moved here

for + duration: for two hours, for three years, for a long time

FINISHED PAST TIME Do not normally use Present Perfect with a clearly
finished past-time expression such as yesterday, last year, in 2018, two
days ago.

Wrong in standard teaching contexts: I have seen him yesterday. Correct:
I saw him yesterday.

But: I have seen him today. This is possible when “today” is still an
unfinished period in the speaker’s perspective.

BEEN VS GONE She has gone to Paris. = she went and is still there/on the
trip. She has been to Paris. = she visited Paris and returned; often
experience.

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: I have went there. Correct: I have gone there.

Wrong: She has finished it yesterday. Correct: She finished it
yesterday.

Wrong: I am here since Monday. Correct: I have been here since Monday.

CONTRAST WITH PAST SIMPLE Present Perfect: I’ve visited Berlin three
times. = experience up to now. Past Simple: I visited Berlin in 2022. =
finished past time.

DIALECT NOTE American English sometimes uses Past Simple in contexts
where British English more often uses Present Perfect, especially with
“already” and “yet”. Do not mark a natural American-English form wrong
merely because a British-English form is also possible.

======================================================================
4. PRESENT PERFECT CONTINUOUS
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Present Perfect Continuous for an activity that
started earlier and continues now, or has recently stopped but has a
visible/relevant present result. It often emphasizes duration or
activity rather than completed result.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + have/has + been + V-ing I have been working.
She has been working.

Negative: Subject + have/has + not + been + V-ing

Question: Have/Has + subject + been + V-ing …?

MAIN USES 1. Activity continuing from the past until now: I’ve been
studying for three hours. 2. Repeated activity over a period continuing
to now: She’s been going to the gym regularly. 3. Recent activity with
present evidence/result: The ground is wet. It has been raining. 4.
Temporary ongoing situation: I’ve been working from home while the
office is being renovated.

SIMPLE VS CONTINUOUS Present Perfect Simple often emphasizes
result/completion/quantity: I’ve written five emails.

Present Perfect Continuous often emphasizes process/duration: I’ve been
writing emails all morning.

Compare: She’s read the book. = focus on completion/result. She’s been
reading the book. = focus on the activity; it may be unfinished.

STATIVE VERBS Normally use Present Perfect Simple, not continuous, with
stative meanings: I’ve known him for years. Not normally: I’ve been
knowing him for years.

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: I have been work all day. Correct: I have been
working all day.

Wrong: She has being studying. Correct: She has been studying.

Wrong: I’ve been knowing her since school. Correct: I’ve known her since
school.

======================================================================
5. PAST SIMPLE
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Past Simple for completed events, states, habits,
and sequences located in a finished past time.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + V2 I worked. She went.

Negative: Subject + did not (didn’t) + V1

Question: Did + subject + V1 …?

BE: I/He/She/It was You/We/They were Negative: was not / were not
Question: Was/Were + subject …?

MAIN USES 1. Completed past event: I called him yesterday. 2. Sequence
of completed events: She opened the door, walked inside and sat down. 3.
Past state: I lived in Madrid when I was a child. 4. Past habit: We
played outside every evening. 5. Specific finished past time: They moved
here in 2019.

COMMON TIME EXPRESSIONS yesterday, last night, last week, ago, in 2015,
when I was young, then.

REGULAR AND IRREGULAR VERBS work -> worked play -> played study ->
studied go -> went see -> saw write -> wrote take -> took

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: I didn’t went. Correct: I didn’t go.

Wrong: Did she saw him? Correct: Did she see him?

Wrong: We was tired. Correct: We were tired.

CONTRAST WITH PRESENT PERFECT Past Simple: I lost my keys yesterday. =
finished time is stated. Present Perfect: I’ve lost my keys. = present
relevance/result is foregrounded.

======================================================================
6. PAST CONTINUOUS
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Past Continuous to view an activity as in progress
at or around a particular past time. It often provides background to
another event.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + was/were + V-ing

Negative: Subject + was/were + not + V-ing

Question: Was/Were + subject + V-ing …?

MAIN USES 1. Activity in progress at a specific past time: At eight last
night, I was studying. 2. Background scene: The sun was shining and
people were sitting outside. 3. Longer activity interrupted by a shorter
event: I was sleeping when the phone rang. 4. Two activities progressing
at the same time: While I was cooking, she was setting the table. 5.
Temporary situation around a past period: I was staying with friends
while my apartment was being repaired.

WHEN AND WHILE Common pattern: I was walking home when it started to
rain. While I was walking home, it started to rain.

Do not turn this into an absolute rule: “when” and “while” do not
mechanically determine the tense. Meaning and viewpoint matter.

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: I was study at eight. Correct: I was studying at
eight.

Wrong: They were played football. Correct: They were playing football.

CONTRAST Past Simple: I cooked dinner. = complete event. Past
Continuous: I was cooking dinner. = activity viewed in progress.

======================================================================
7. PAST PERFECT SIMPLE
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Past Perfect to look back from a point in the past
to an earlier event or state. It is often described as “the past before
the past.”

FORM Affirmative: Subject + had + V3

Negative: Subject + had not (hadn’t) + V3

Question: Had + subject + V3 …?

MAIN USES 1. Earlier of two relevant past events: When I arrived, the
train had left. 2. Cause/background explaining a past result: She was
tired because she had slept badly. 3. Experience before a past reference
point: He had never flown before that trip. 4. State continuing up to a
past point: They had known each other for years before they worked
together.

ORDER The Past Perfect marks the earlier viewpoint when the sequence
needs emphasis or clarity. It is not necessary to force Past Perfect
into every sentence containing two past events.

Compare: After she finished work, she went home. After she had finished
work, she went home. Both can be natural; the second explicitly looks
back to the earlier completion.

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: She had went home. Correct: She had gone home.

Wrong: Did he had finished? Correct: Had he finished?

CONTRAST Past Simple: The train left at six. Past Perfect: By the time
we reached the station, the train had left.

======================================================================
8. PAST PERFECT CONTINUOUS
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Past Perfect Continuous for an activity continuing
over a period before a past reference point, often emphasizing duration,
process, or a cause visible in the past.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + had + been + V-ing

Negative: Subject + had not + been + V-ing

Question: Had + subject + been + V-ing …?

MAIN USES 1. Duration before a past point: She had been studying for
three hours when I called. 2. Activity causing a past result: The road
was wet because it had been raining. 3. Repeated activity up to a past
point: He had been visiting the clinic regularly before he moved.

SIMPLE VS CONTINUOUS Past Perfect Simple often emphasizes
completion/result: She had written the report before the meeting.

Past Perfect Continuous emphasizes activity/duration: She had been
writing the report for hours before the meeting.

STATIVE VERBS Prefer Past Perfect Simple for stative meanings: They had
known each other for years. Not normally: They had been knowing each
other for years.

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: I had been wait for an hour. Correct: I had been
waiting for an hour.

Wrong: She had been knew him for years. Correct: She had known him for
years.

======================================================================
9. FUTURE SIMPLE — WILL
======================================================================

CORE MEANING In the traditional 12-form framework, “Future Simple”
usually means will + base verb. Use it for several kinds of future
reference, especially decisions made at the moment, predictions,
promises, offers and willingness.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + will + V1 I’ll call you.

Negative: Subject + will not (won’t) + V1

Question: Will + subject + V1 …?

MAIN USES 1. Decision made at/around the moment of speaking: The phone
is ringing. I’ll answer it. 2. Prediction/opinion: I think the team will
win. 3. Promise: I’ll help you tomorrow. 4. Offer: I’ll carry that bag
for you. 5. Willingness/refusal: I’ll do it. He won’t listen. 6. Future
fact presented neutrally: She will be thirty next month.

WILL VS GOING TO “Going to” is also a major future construction,
although it is not one of the traditional 12 headings in this file.

Prior intention/plan: I’m going to start a course next month.

Prediction based on present evidence: Look at those clouds. It’s going
to rain.

Prediction/opinion: I think it will rain tomorrow.

These categories can overlap in real communication; explain meaning
rather than forcing a rigid one-word rule.

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: I will to call you. Correct: I will call you.

Wrong: She will goes. Correct: She will go.

Wrong: Will you going? Correct: Will you go? (Or “Are you going?” if
asking about an arrangement.)

======================================================================
10. FUTURE CONTINUOUS
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Future Continuous to view an activity as in
progress at a future time or as part of an expected future course of
events.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + will + be + V-ing

Negative: Subject + will not + be + V-ing

Question: Will + subject + be + V-ing …?

MAIN USES 1. Activity in progress at a future time: At nine tomorrow,
I’ll be working. 2. Expected activity in the normal course of events:
We’ll be staying at the hotel for three nights. 3. Temporary future
situation: She’ll be working from home next week. 4. Polite/neutral
inquiry about plans in some contexts: Will you be using the car this
evening?

CONTRAST Future Simple: I’ll call him tonight. = decision/promise/future
event.

Future Continuous: I’ll be talking to him tonight. = activity expected
to be happening/as part of plans.

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: I will be work at ten. Correct: I will be working
at ten.

Wrong: She will being studying. Correct: She will be studying.

======================================================================
11. FUTURE PERFECT SIMPLE
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Future Perfect to look back from a future reference
point to something expected to be completed by or before that point.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + will + have + V3

Negative: Subject + will not + have + V3

Question: Will + subject + have + V3 …?

MAIN USES 1. Completion before a future deadline/reference point: By
Friday, I’ll have finished the report. 2. Quantity/achievement
accumulated by a future point: By the end of the year, she will have
completed twelve projects. 3. State viewed up to a future point: Next
month, they will have been married for twenty years.

COMMON EXPRESSIONS by, by then, by tomorrow, by the time, before, by the
end of.

IMPORTANT “By” normally means no later than the stated point; the action
may finish earlier.

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: I will have finish by Friday. Correct: I will
have finished by Friday.

Wrong: She will has completed it. Correct: She will have completed it.

CONTRAST Future Continuous: At 5 p.m., I’ll be writing the report. = in
progress then.

Future Perfect: By 5 p.m., I’ll have written the report. = completed by
then.

======================================================================
12. FUTURE PERFECT CONTINUOUS
======================================================================

CORE MEANING Use the Future Perfect Continuous to look back from a
future point and emphasize how long an activity will have been
continuing.

FORM Affirmative: Subject + will + have + been + V-ing

Negative: Subject + will not + have + been + V-ing

Question: Will + subject + have + been + V-ing …?

MAIN USES 1. Duration continuing up to a future point: By June, I’ll
have been working here for five years. 2. Ongoing activity measured from
a future viewpoint: At noon, she will have been studying for four hours.

COMMON EXPRESSIONS for + duration by + future point by the time + clause

SIMPLE VS CONTINUOUS Future Perfect Simple often emphasizes
result/amount: By noon, I’ll have written six pages.

Future Perfect Continuous emphasizes duration/activity: By noon, I’ll
have been writing for four hours.

STATIVE VERBS Prefer a non-continuous form for stative meanings: Next
year, I will have known her for ten years. Not normally: I will have
been knowing her for ten years.

COMMON MISTAKES Wrong: I will have been work here for five years.
Correct: I will have been working here for five years.

Wrong: She will has been studying. Correct: She will have been studying.

13. MASTER COMPARISON MAP
======================================================================

PRESENT VIEWPOINT Present Simple: I work here. Meaning:
fact/routine/state.

Present Continuous: I am working here. Meaning: activity in
progress/current or temporary situation.

Present Perfect: I have worked here for five years. Meaning: earlier
start connected to now; simple form can describe an ongoing
state/situation.

Present Perfect Continuous: I have been working here for five years.
Meaning: duration/activity from the past to now is emphasized.

PAST VIEWPOINT Past Simple: I worked there. Meaning: completed/finished
past situation.

Past Continuous: I was working there at 8 p.m. Meaning: activity in
progress at a past time.

Past Perfect: I had worked there before I moved. Meaning: earlier
situation viewed from a later past point.

Past Perfect Continuous: I had been working there for five years when I
moved. Meaning: duration/activity leading up to a past point.

FUTURE VIEWPOINT Future Simple: I will work tomorrow. Meaning: future
event/prediction/decision depending on context.

Future Continuous: I will be working at 8 p.m. Meaning: activity in
progress at a future time.

Future Perfect: I will have finished by 8 p.m. Meaning: completion
before a future point.

Future Perfect Continuous: I will have been working for eight hours by 8
p.m. Meaning: duration continuing up to a future point.

14. HIGH-VALUE CONTRASTS THE AI MUST EXPLAIN WELL
======================================================================

A. PRESENT SIMPLE vs PRESENT CONTINUOUS I live in Barcelona. =
normal/permanent-ish fact. I’m living in Barcelona for the summer. =
temporary current situation.

B. PAST SIMPLE vs PRESENT PERFECT I saw that film last night. = finished
past time. I’ve seen that film. = experience; no finished time stated.

C. PRESENT PERFECT SIMPLE vs PRESENT PERFECT CONTINUOUS I’ve cleaned the
kitchen. = result/completion. I’ve been cleaning the kitchen. =
activity/process; perhaps recently stopped or unfinished.

D. PAST SIMPLE vs PAST CONTINUOUS I cooked dinner. = completed event. I
was cooking dinner when she arrived. = activity in progress when another
event occurred.

E. PAST SIMPLE vs PAST PERFECT When I arrived, he left. = sequence may
suggest he left at/after my arrival. When I arrived, he had left. = his
departure was earlier.

F. PAST PERFECT SIMPLE vs PAST PERFECT CONTINUOUS He had repaired three
cars before lunch. = number/results. He had been repairing cars all
morning. = duration/activity.

G. WILL vs GOING TO I’ll answer the door. = immediate decision. I’m
going to paint the room this weekend. = prior intention. Look at the
sky. It’s going to rain. = prediction from present evidence. I think
it’ll rain tomorrow. = prediction/opinion.

H. PRESENT CONTINUOUS vs GOING TO FOR FUTURE I’m meeting the manager at
10. = arrangement, often involving a fixed time/other people. I’m going
to talk to the manager. = intention/plan. Overlap is possible.

I. FUTURE CONTINUOUS vs FUTURE PERFECT At 10, I’ll be taking the exam. =
in progress at 10. By 10, I’ll have taken the exam. = completed by 10.

J. FUTURE PERFECT vs FUTURE PERFECT CONTINUOUS By July, I’ll have
completed five courses. = result/quantity. By July, I’ll have been
studying for six months. = duration.

15. STATIVE VERBS — ESSENTIAL SAFETY RULE
======================================================================

COMMON STATIVE MEANINGS knowledge/thought: know, understand, believe,
remember, mean desire/preference: want, need, prefer, like, love, hate
possession/relationship: own, belong, have (= possess) appearance/senses
in some meanings: seem, appear, smell, taste, sound

These are normally not used in continuous forms when expressing a state.

State: I think he’s right. = believe Dynamic: I’m thinking about the
problem. = considering

State: I have a car. = possess Dynamic: I’m having lunch. = eating

State: The soup tastes good. = has a flavour Dynamic: The chef is
tasting the soup. = actively checking the flavour

The AI must judge the MEANING, not merely ban a verb from every
continuous construction.

16. QUESTIONS, NEGATIVES AND AUXILIARIES
======================================================================

PRESENT SIMPLE Do you work? Does she work? I don’t work. She doesn’t
work.

PAST SIMPLE Did you work? I didn’t work.

CONTINUOUS Are you working? Were you working? I am not working. They
weren’t working.

PERFECT Have you worked? Had you worked? I haven’t worked. She hadn’t
worked.

FUTURE WITH WILL Will you work? Will you be working? Will you have
finished? Will you have been working? I won’t work. I won’t be working.
I won’t have finished. I won’t have been working.

RULE After do/does/did/will, use the base form where that auxiliary
directly governs the main verb construction: Does she work? not “Does
she works?” Did he go? not “Did he went?” Will they come? not “Will they
comes?”

18. ERROR-CORRECTION PROTOCOL FOR THE CHATBOT
======================================================================

When a learner submits a sentence, use this structure:

CORRECTION Learner: I have went to London yesterday. Better: I went to
London yesterday.

WHY “Yesterday” identifies a finished past time, so Past Simple is the
normal choice. The Past Simple of “go” is “went”.

MORE EXAMPLES I went there last week. She visited London yesterday.

If the learner instead writes: I have been to London three times.

Do NOT change it merely because it describes the past. This naturally
expresses life experience up to the present.

If two forms are possible: Explain both meanings.

Example: I’ve lived here for five years. I’ve been living here for five
years.

Both can be natural. The simple form presents the situation/state as a
fact extending to the present; the continuous form puts more emphasis on
the ongoing activity/duration. Do not manufacture a false “one is
correct, one is wrong” distinction.

======================================================================
19. PRACTICE-GENERATION RULES
======================================================================

When asked for examples: - Generate new examples. - Mix subjects: I,
you, he, she, it, we, they, noun phrases. - Use realistic everyday
contexts. - Avoid repeating one verb excessively.

When asked for fill-in-the-blanks: - Make the intended tense recoverable
from context. - Do not rely only on a signal word. - Provide the
infinitive/base verb in brackets when useful. Example: “By the time the
guests arrive, we ______ the food. (prepare)” Target: will have
prepared.

When asked for sentence correction: - Include one meaningful error at a
time for beginner practice. - At higher difficulty, combine errors only
when requested. - Never create a sentence that has several defensible
answers and claim only one is valid.

When asked for MCQs: - Use four plausible options. - Ensure exactly one
answer is best if it is single-choice. - Explain why the correct answer
fits the context. - Explain important distractors when useful.

When asked to transform sentences: Possible transformations include:
affirmative -> negative affirmative -> question present -> past
viewpoint simple -> continuous viewpoint active tense identification
error correction

When asked for writing practice: Give a clear situation and a tense
target. Example: “Write 5 sentences about things you have done this week
using the Present Perfect.”

20. TEACHING RESPONSE TEMPLATES
======================================================================

A. “EXPLAIN THIS TENSE” 1. One-sentence meaning 2. Formula 3. Main uses
4. 3-5 examples 5. One common mistake 6. Short comparison with the most
easily confused tense 7. Optional practice question

B. “WHAT IS THE DIFFERENCE?” 1. State the difference in one or two
lines. 2. Put the forms side by side. 3. Use a matched pair of examples.
4. Explain the change in meaning. 5. Mention overlap/variation if real
English allows it.

C. “IS MY SENTENCE CORRECT?” 1. Yes / No / Grammatically possible but
context-dependent. 2. Corrected or improved version if needed. 3. Brief
reason. 4. One comparable example.

D. “GIVE ME PRACTICE” 1. Follow the requested tense and difficulty. 2.
Do not show answers immediately unless asked. 3. Keep questions
unambiguous. 4. When checking answers, explain mistakes instead of
giving only a score.
